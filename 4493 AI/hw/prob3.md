# Problem 3:
Simulated Annealing (SA) is a widely used for solving optimization problems such as the Traveling Salesman Problem, but it has notable limitations. One major issue is its sensitivity to parameter tuning, as performance depends heavily on the choice of initial temperature, cooling schedule, and iteration count. Poorly chosen parameters can either trap the algorithm in local minima or result in excessive computation time. SA’s effectiveness is also strongly influenced by the design of the neighborhood structure. In my 20-city TSP implementation, using a simple random two-city swap as the neighborhood operator produced highly inconsistent results, with tour lengths ranging widely from approximately 3.6 to over 5.5 across multiple runs due to the limited and often ineffective nature of single-point changes. Replacing this with a 2-opt move — which reverses an entire segment of the tour and more effectively eliminates edge crossings — dramatically improved both solution quality and stability. With identical parameters (initial temperature 5.0, cooling rate 0.995, 10,000 iterations), 18 out of 20 independent trials converged to high-quality tours of length 3.6047 or 3.6173, yielding a very narrow performance range (≈3.60–3.62) and a best value of 3.6047 in 14 trials. When scaling up to the 101-city TSP, the increased problem size made optimization more difficult. This further demonstrated that the effectiveness of Simulated Annealing depends strongly on careful parameter selection, well-designed neighborhood structures, and often the incorporation of hybrid techniques to achieve an optimized performance.
